Verification as Identity: Accountability, Personhood and the Scarcity of
Verification in the Agent Economy
Wulf A. Kaal1
Abstract
The Kaal Corpus argues that verification becomes the scarce input when agent intelligence
is abundant and that deployer liability is the transitional rule for harm (Kaal 2026c, 2026g,
2026l). It states, in one sentence and by legal stipulation, that a persistent, non-transferable
reputation record gives an agent an identity (Kaal 2026b, 2026l), but it does not develop the
point. This Article develops it and adds what the corpus does not contain. It treats identity
as a linkage rule that maps agent instances to records, shows that the rule’s error rates set
the agent’s capacity to answer for harm, and states the minimal linkage at which an agent can
answer for a given harm. It derives a model in which an agent holding such a record answers
for harm from its own earnings and exit incentives, with the threshold standing at which a
principal’s exposure falls to zero and two regimes of failure: a cliff, in which the agent exits
and the injured party recovers nothing, and graceful degradation, in which the agent stays and
pays what it can. It extends the model to strategic claimants, partial inheritance, correlated
harm, an endogenous entry cost, verifier incentives and sponsorship chains, and it states the
stake at which verifiers are honest, the places where the regress of verifiers ends, and the limits
of sponsorship. It reports two registered tests of the decision rule, the first of which failed on a
specification error that the Article corrects. It compares the record with existing agent-identity
standards and reports a descriptive study of 1,156 feedback events in the ERC-8004 registries,
which finds almost no evidence attached and no consequence. A documented search found no
work that combines these elements. The evidence is limited. The tests use a prompted language
model with no stake, the registry data cover one chain in one window, and the novelty search
was not independent.
Nature and scope of claims. This Article states a hypothesis about design opportunities. It
makes no claim about the content of existing law, and it makes no normative claim about what any
legal system should provide. Terms such as personhood, liability, principal and identity are used as
design vocabulary. The propositions are conditional. The evidence for the underlying mechanisms
is partial and is stated as such in Part VI.
Keywords: agent economy; verification; reputation; agent identity; accountability; agency costs;
computative economics; institutional design

I. Introduction
When an agent causes harm today, the consequence is assigned to someone else. In Moffatt v. Air
Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, a British Columbia tribunal held the airline liable for a misstatement
made by its website chatbot and rejected the submission that the chatbot was a separate legal
entity (WeirFoulds LLP 2024). Andrew D’Souza (2026) argues that this arrangement caps what
agents can do, because trust attaches to the principal, and agents multiply without multiplying
accountable parties.
1
The Kaal Corpus has developed the argument that verification becomes scarce, that posted collat-
eral does not scale deterrence, and that deployer liability remains the transitional rule (Kaal 2026c,
2026g, 2026j, 2026l). It states that a persistent, non-transferable record gives an agent an identity
whose history cannot be shed costlessly (Kaal 2026b), and it resolves agent identity by stipulation,
taking the legal subject to be the registered record (Kaal 2026l). No paper in the corpus develops
that point. This Article develops it and then asks the question the corpus leaves open: under
what conditions can an agent that holds such a record answer for a harm of a given size, and what
remains for a human or organizational principal when it cannot?
The Article makes six contributions. First, a development of identity from a record: identity is
a rule that links agent instances to records, the rule’s error rates set the agent’s capacity, and
the Article states the minimal linkage at which an agent can answer for a given harm (Part IV).
Second, a model that gives the answerability capacity of an agent, the threshold standing at which
the principal’s exposure is zero, and the two regimes, cliff and graceful degradation, in which
recovery fails (Part V). Third, a model of sponsorship chains that shows how judgment-proof gaps
compound (Part V.J), and extensions to strategic claimants, partial record inheritance, correlated
harm, an endogenous entry cost and verifier incentives, including the stake at which verifiers are
honest (Part V). Fourth, a comparison of the record with existing agent-identity standards (Part
II.B). Fifth, two registered tests of the decision rule and a descriptive study of real registry data
(Part VII, Appendices A and D). Sixth, a documented search for prior statements of the chain,
with its limits (Part II.D, Appendix C). Part II places the Article against the corpus, the standards,
the prior work and the doctrine. Part III distinguishes what the corpus develops from what it only
states. Part IV develops identity from a record. Part V states the model. Part VI states boundary
conditions. Part VII states the implications and the tests. Part VIII concludes.

B. Existing standards for agent and software identity
Several standards and proposals already identify agents or software, and the question is what each
binds to. This Part compares them with the record that Part IV describes. The comparison rests
on the primary specifications as retrieved on 14 November 2026, and it states only what those
documents say.
Decentralized Identifiers. A DID is an identifier that enables verifiable, decentralized digital identity
and can refer to any subject (W3C 2022). The specification defines identifiers, DID documents,
verification methods and their revocation. It defines no performance record, and it does not use
the terms reputation or track record.
Verifiable Credentials. A verifiable credential is a way to express a set of claims made by an issuer
(W3C 2025). The data model can carry an issuer’s attestation about a subject. It leaves out of
scope why a verifier trusts an issuer, noting only that some issuers may be trusted because of their
reputation. A verification result could be expressed as a credential, but the standard does not say
what is verified or who verifies.
SPIFFE. SPIFFE is a set of open-source standards for securely identifying software systems in
dynamic and heterogeneous environments (SPIFFE n.d.). It identifies workloads for mutual au-
thentication. It addresses who the software is, not how well it has performed.
A2A Agent Card. The Agent2Agent protocol, in version 1.0.0, defines an Agent Card that declares
an agent’s provider, capabilities, skills, interfaces and security schemes, and it provides for signing
the card (A2A Protocol n.d.). The card is a self-declared description. It is not a record of verified
work.
C2PA. The C2PA specification, version 2.2, provides content provenance and authenticity for media
assets (C2PA n.d.). Its object is the asset. It does not describe an agent’s history.
Sigstore. Sigstore addresses software signing, a mitigation for software supply-chain compromise
(Newman, Meyers and Torres-Arias 2022). Its object is the signed artifact.
ERC-8004. The closest proposal is ERC-8004, “Trustless Agents,” a draft Ethereum standard
created on 13 August 2025 (De Rossi et al. 2025). It defines three registries. The Identity Registry
uses ERC-721 tokens as agent handles. The specification states that the owner of the token owns
the agent and can transfer ownership. The Reputation Registry lets any client give feedback on an
agent, with tags and a value, and lets the submitter revoke it. Scoring and aggregation occur largely
off-chain. The Validation Registry provides hooks for independent validators. The specification’s
own security considerations state that “Sybil attacks are possible, inflating the reputation of fake
agents.”
The comparison yields four differences, each limited to the documents read.
Identity as history. The identifier standards bind a name to a key or a subject. The record in Part
IV binds the name to a verified performance history.
Transferability. ERC-8004 identities are transferable. Part IV requires a non-transferable record,
for the reason Tadelis (1999) gives.
3
Adverse states. ERC-8004 feedback can be revoked by its submitter, and revoked feedback is
omitted by default in reads. Part IV requires that adverse findings persist.
Component attribution. None of the documents read scores the agent, its harness and its model
separately.
The author’s research agenda reports a related survey of five verification families, as retrieved at a
cutoff of 20 August 2026, that finds no standard verification output composing who bears a duty,
what is warranted, what collateral is reserved, how contrary evidence is admitted and how reserved
funds are reached (Kaal 2026k). The standards identify and attest. None of the specifications read
defines a verified performance history that carries consequence. The record is a proposed layer
above them, which can reuse their identifiers and credentials as transport.
C. The gap
The literatures meet at a point none addresses alone. Agency theory explains who bears cost when
an agent acts for another. Organizational law explains how an artificial entity can answer with its
own assets. Reputation theory explains why a persistent identity sustains cooperation. Information
economics explains why verification is costly and valuable. No account that the author’s systematic
search found (Appendix C) connects them for agents: it does not state how verification at scale
produces an identity that can carry accountability, nor what follows for the role of the principal.
The identity standards supply identifiers and attestations, and the oversight literature supplies
methods of judging output, but neither supplies a persistent record that carries consequence. This
Article states that connection as a hypothesis and derives, in a formal model, the condition under
which it holds.
D. Closest prior work and the contribution
The following works state parts of the chain, and the Article builds on them. The characterizations
rest on titles, abstracts and the stated subjects of the works, and the Article does not claim to have
exhausted them. On entity and personhood questions, Pagallo (2013) treats the crimes, contracts
and torts of robots, Teubner (2018) addresses the status of autonomous software agents in private

builds on that work to examine entities that algorithms control with no human controllers, Wagner

E. Doctrine
Three doctrines bear on the hypothesis. First, vicarious liability in agency law, respondeat superior
and apparent authority, assigns consequence to a principal because of assent and control, and
not because the principal bears monitoring cost. The Article’s hypothesis therefore concerns a
functional substitute for that assignment and not a change in its legal test. Second, corporate
personhood shows that the law has fixed a subject by rule. Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 17
U.S. (4 Wheat.) 518 (1819), describes the corporation as an artificial being, invisible, intangible
and existing only in contemplation of law, and Salomon v A Salomon and Co Ltd [1897] AC 22
separates the company’s obligations from its members. Asset partitioning, and not personhood
alone, made the corporation answerable (Hansmann and Kraakman 2000), which is the point of
the earnings condition in Part V. Third, Moffatt v Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, held an airline
liable for its chatbot’s misstatement as negligent misrepresentation. It is a small-claims decision
with no precedential force, and it did not turn on agency doctrine. The Article takes no position
on whether any legal system will recognize a registered record as a holder of earnings.
III. The Propositions Attributed to the Corpus
The Article rests on five propositions. For each, the corpus is cited for what it develops, and the
Article says where it supplies the development itself.
Proposition 1: scale requires self-suﬀicient, evolving agents. The corpus supplies the economic
premise (Kaal 2026c). Part V.D derives a bound that depends on finite principal capacity.
Proposition 2: when agent intelligence is abundant, verification is scarce. The corpus develops this
(Kaal 2026c, 2026g).
Proposition 3: verification at scale yields a persistent, non-transferable record that functions as the
agent’s identity. The corpus states the premise in one sentence (Kaal 2026b, Part III.A), resolves
agent identity by legal stipulation (Kaal 2026l), and shows that identity must persist because free
new identities defeat reputation (Kaal 2026f). It does not develop what an identity rule is, how
it treats updates and successors, or how its errors bear on accountability. Part IV supplies that
development.
Proposition 4: identity makes accountability possible. The corpus supports pieces of it: a non-
transferable record gives exposure without set-aside capital (Kaal 2026l), the substrate is designed

IV. Identity from a Record
The corpus states that a persistent, non-transferable reputation record gives an agent an identity
whose history cannot be shed costlessly (Kaal 2026b, Part III.A), and it resolves agent identity by
stipulation, taking the legal subject to be the registered record (Kaal 2026l). This Part develops
what those statements leave open: what it means for a record to be an identity, what rule links
an agent to its record, and how the quality of that rule bears on whether the agent can answer for
harm.
A. What it means for the record to be the identity
An identity rule is a map ι from an agent instance x to a record ι(x). An instance is a signing
key, model weights, a harness, a configuration and a time. Standing and obligations follow ι. The
record is the agent’s identity when counterparties condition on ι(x) and on nothing else about x.
Identity in this sense is a rule, and not a property of the agent. It does not claim that the agent
persists in any metaphysical sense, which is the position of Kaal (2026l). It claims only that the
thing a counterparty consults, and the thing that bears consequence, is fixed by the rule.
Five requirements make the record usable as the referent of accountability.
Binding: an instance maps to at most one record at a time.
Persistence: the record survives across transactions.
Non-transferability: the record cannot be separated from the instance by sale or loan, because a
transferable record lets capital-rich but competence-poor actors buy reputation and so destroys the
signal (Tadelis 1999; Kaal 2026a).
Continuity under change: legitimate change, such as a learning update, a harness swap or a model
upgrade, keeps the record.
Consequence: an adverse finding attaches and stays. The corpus assumes the first three. The last
two are where design is needed, and they conflict. An agent that evolves must change, and a rule
6
that tolerates change also tolerates evasion.
B. The linkage problem and what its errors cost
Three kinds of successor instance can face the rule. A legitimate successor is the same agent updated
in the ordinary course. An evasive successor is an instance that exits to shed an obligation. An
independent successor is an unrelated new agent. The rule links an evasive successor to the old
record with probability λ, wrongly splits a legitimate successor from its record with probability ϵ
per period, and wrongly merges an independent newcomer with an old record with probability µ.
The parameter λ is the linkage of Result 7, so the identity rule is the linkage parameter, and its
quality determines capacity.

C. The unit of identity: agent, harness and model
The record can attach to a key, to the bundle of key, harness and model, or to each component.
A record keyed only to the signing key keeps ϵ near zero, but a swap of model or harness changes
the agent’s behavior under the same record, so the record misdescribes the instance. A record
keyed to a behavioral signature describes the instance better, but a legitimate update risks a split.
Component records, scored separately for the agent, the harness and the model, let a failure be
attributed to its source, and they raise the cost of evasion, because an evasive successor must
change all three to escape. They also multiply the number of linkage decisions, each with its own
error rates. The Article offers no argument for how the components are identified separately from
outcomes alone, and that question remains open.
This is where the objection of Hu, Rong and Van Kleek (2026) bites. They argue that language-
model agents lack the grounding that reputation mechanisms require, because one model is in-
stantiated many times in different contexts and a record keyed to weights merges what should be
separate. A record keyed only to context splits what should merge. Grounding is the identity rule,
and the rule is a design choice, not a fact to be discovered.
D. What Part IV adds and what it does not
Part IV adds four things to the corpus. Identity is a rule, and not a property of the agent. The
rule has three error rates that can be named. Capacity depends on them in closed form (Result
13). And identity is necessary and not suﬀicient for accountability, because the earnings bound
remains. It does not estimate the error rates, and it does not derive the effect of wrongful merging.
It leaves enforcement of non-transferability against the sale of keys outside the model, and it treats
the rule as an institutional or legal choice and not a discovery (Kaal 2026l).
V. A Formal Model of Answerability
This Part derives the conditions under which an agent can answer for harm from its own earnings
and exit incentives. It formalizes Propositions 1 and 4. The model is deliberately minimal, and its
results are conditional on the assumptions of Part V.A. All mathematics is stated in LaTeX. Proofs
are in Appendix B.
A. Setup
An agent holds a record of standing R ≥ 0 in a domain. Its earnings per period are w(R) = w0 +κR,
where w0 ≥ 0 is the earnings of an unverified identity and κ> 0 is the increase in earnings per unit
of standing. The agent discounts at δ ∈ (0, 1), so that
V (R) = w0 + κR 1 − δ , V0 = V (0).
8
An output of the agent causes harm of size D> 0, measured as the present value of the obligation
at adjudication, and the harm is adjudicated. The record is slashed to R+ = (1−s)R with s ∈ [0, 1),
and write V + = V (R+). The agent owes D. The unpaid balance accrues at the discount rate, and
the obligation is collected by garnishing a share φ ∈ (0, 1] of per-period earnings until it is paid.
The most that collection can ever yield is therefore
E = φV +.
The agent has two options. It can stay: keep the record and let the obligation be collected. Or it
can exit: abandon the record, pay a one-time cost c ≥ 0 to start again under a new identity, and
earn w0 thereafter. The obligation attaches to the record and does not follow the new identity. This
assumption encodes Proposition 3, that identity is the persistent record, and the results depend on
it. Part V.F relaxes it. Define the exit-side capacity
X = V + − V0 + c = κ(1 − s)R 1 − δ + c,
and the answerability capacity
Γ(R,c) = min{E,X}.
Ties are resolved in favor of staying.
B. Decision, recovery and answerability
Result 1 (decision rule). After an adjudicated harm of size D, the agent stays if and only if
min{D,E} ≤ X, equivalently D ≤ X or X ≥ E.
If it stays, the injured party recovers ρ(D) = min{D,E}. If it exits, the injured party recovers
ρ(D) = 0.
Result 2 (answerability and the shape of recovery). The injured party recovers D in full if and only
if D ≤ Γ(R,c). Define the entry-cost threshold
¯c = max{0, E − (V + − V0)}.
If c ≥ ¯c, then X ≥ E, the agent never exits, and recovery degrades gracefully: ρ(D) = min{D,E}.
If c < ¯c, recovery has a cliff: ρ(D) = D for D ≤ X and ρ(D) = 0 for D > X. A record can
therefore fail to answer for a large harm in two different ways. Where entry is costly, the injured
party receives the whole of the agent’s garnishable earnings. Where entry is cheap, the injured
party receives nothing, because the agent leaves.
Remark. An earlier version of this Article stated that the agent honors if and only if D ≤ Γ.
That statement conflated full answerability with staying. It was wrong for D > E when X ≥ E,
9
where the agent stays and pays what it can. The error was found when a registered test (Part VII,
Appendix A) failed on its third hypothesis, and Result 1 is the corrected rule.
C. Threshold standing and comparative statics
Result 3 (threshold standing). In a domain with maximum harm ¯D, the agent can answer in full
for every harm if and only if R ≥ R∗( ¯D), where
R∗ = max{0, RE , RX }, RE = (1 − δ) ¯D/φ − w0 κ(1 − s) , RX = (1 − δ)( ¯D − c) κ(1 − s) .
The earnings condition E ≥ ¯D holds if and only if R ≥ RE . The exit condition X ≥ ¯D holds if
and only if R ≥ RX . Moreover, Γ is nondecreasing in R, φ, δ, c, κ and w0 and nonincreasing in s.
The threshold R∗ is nonincreasing in φ, δ, c, κ and w0 and nondecreasing in ¯D and s.
Example. Take w0 = 1, κ = 1, δ = 0.95, s = 0.5 and φ = 1, so that V (R) = 20(1 + R). For
¯D = 100, the threshold is R∗ = 10 when c = 0, because the exit condition binds, and R∗ = 8 when
c = 50, because the earnings condition binds. If only half of earnings can be garnished, φ = 0.5,
then R∗ = 18 in both cases. The threshold is finite for every bounded harm, but where ¯D is large
relative to w0 and κ it can exceed any standing an agent could accumulate in practice, and the
principal’s exposure then remains.
Result 4 (deterrence cap). Suppose the agent chooses care at cost kc per task, which lowers the
probability of harm from q0 to q1, and a harm is adjudicated with probability a. The agent’s loss
from an adjudicated harm is
L(D,R) = min{ V (R) − V + + min{D,E}, V (R) − V0 + c },
which is bounded by κR/(1 − δ) + c however large D is. The agent exercises care if and only if
a(q0 − q1)L ≥ kc. For harms larger than Γ the agent’s own exposure stops rising with the harm,
so the agent is under-deterred, and the shortfall must be supplied by another party. This is the
sense in which the principal’s role is transitional and not obsolete. A refundable bond B that the
agent recovers on exit lowers the exit-side capacity to X − B. Refundable collateral therefore does
not expand, and can contract, the harm an agent can answer for. A bond that is forfeited on an
adjudicated harm has the opposite effect: it adds B to the earnings-side capacity, which becomes
E + B. The sign of the effect of collateral therefore depends on whether the claimant can reach it,
and the conclusion is limited to collateral that the claimant cannot reach. This agrees in direction
with the delegated-staking analysis (Kaal 2026j).
D. Scale: a derivation of Proposition 1
Result 5 (scale bound). Consider a regime in which a human or organizational principal must stand
behind every harm the agent cannot answer for. Each agent performs m tasks per period, and
harm per task is drawn from a distribution F on [0, ¯D]. A task whose harm exceeds Γ requires
10
τ units of principal attention, and principals supply M units per period in total. The expected
attention demand of N agents is Nmτψ, where ψ = 1 − F (Γ). The regime is feasible if and only if
N ≤ Nmax(Γ) = M mτψ(Γ) .
The bound Nmax is finite whenever Γ < ¯D, nondecreasing in Γ, and infinite if and only if Γ ≥ ¯D,
that is, if and only if R ≥ R∗( ¯D). Principal attention then no longer bounds the number of agents.
The bound passes to verification throughput. If each unit of standing requires ν units of verification
and verification capacity is G per period, then N agents reach R∗ in time T = NνR∗/G, so that
Nmax(T ) = GT/(νR∗).
This derives Proposition 1 as a conditional result. Scale is bounded by principal attention while
agents cannot answer for harm, and by verification throughput once they can. The derivation
depends on the exposure rule stated at the start of this Result and on the parameters M , τ , m
and ν, which are not calibrated. It also treats principal capacity as attention. A large deployer
or insurer can stand behind many agents through capital, so the premise of Proposition 1 holds
only to the extent that capital and insurability are themselves finite, which is a further assumption
(Priest 1985).
E. Strategic adjudication
Result 6 (claimant participation). Suppose the injured party bears litigation cost f and a claim
that is brought is adjudicated successfully with probability a. The claimant sues if and only if
aρ(D) ≥ f . Hence, if c ≥ ¯c, the claim is brought if and only if D ≥ f/a and E ≥ f/a. If
c < ¯c, the claim is brought if and only if f/a ≤ D ≤ X. Harms below f/a are never remedied
however large the agent’s capacity. In the cliff regime, harms above X are never brought, so they
are neither remedied nor slashed, and deterrence vanishes where it is most needed. The remedy
suggested by the model is adjudication that does not depend on the claimant’s recovery, such as a
verifier-initiated slash of the record.

H. Endogenous entry cost

J. Sponsorship chains: where judgment-proof gaps compound
Proposition 5 says that accountable agents can sponsor other agents and that the sponsor answers
for the work it commissions. Two questions follow: how much harm a chain of agents can answer
for, and how many sub-agents one sponsor can stand behind. Order a chain of m agents from the
agent that performs the work (level 1) to the final sponsor (level m). An obligation that one level
does not discharge passes to the next level, which owes it as sponsor. Each level is an agent as in
Part V.A, with its own E_l and X_l, and decides by Result 1 whether to stay when the obligation
reaches it.
Result 11 (chain recovery). Let r1 = D and rl+1 = rl − ρl(rl), where ρl is the recovery of Result 2
at level l. The chain recovers D − rm+1. (a) If every level has cl ≥ ¯cl, recovery degrades gracefully
and the chain recovers min{D, ∑ l El}, so capacities add. (b) If every level has cl < ¯cl, the chain
recovers D in full if D ≤ maxl Xl and nothing otherwise, so capacities do not add: a chain of cliff
agents is only as good as its best single member. (c) In a mixed chain the order matters, because
a graceful level lowers the residual that a cliff level above it faces.
Adding sponsors therefore adds nothing when exit is cheap, because each agent in turn leaves when
the obligation reaches it. A sponsor’s guarantee is worth the sum of its parts only where exit is
costly.

K. Design implications
The results suggest a capacity-gated admission rule. An agent should be admitted to a task whose
maximum harm is ¯D only if Γ(R,c) ≥ ¯D, or if a deployer or insurer bonds the shortfall ¯D − Γ.
This extends the principle that standing determines what an agent may validate and stake (Kaal
2026b). Three further implications follow. Entry cost should be set at ¯c and not above it (Result
9). Adjudication should not depend on the claimant’s recovery (Result 6). Registries should treat
linkage across identities as the principal design parameter (Result 7).
L. Scope and limits of the model
The model is a single-agent, deterministic, stationary model. It assumes that adjudication is
accurate, that the agent has no assets beyond its earnings, which is the judgment-proof case, and
that harms are adjudicated at a known present value. It also assumes that the agent holds the
earnings it is garnished from, and that an enforcer outside the model attaches the obligation to
the record. If earnings pass to an operator, the operator is the residual claimant and the principal
remains. The model therefore presupposes a rule that treats the registered record as a holder of
earnings, which is the personhood question that the Article does not resolve. Result 5 depends
on an exposure rule and on uncalibrated parameters. Results 6 to 12 extend the model along
the dimensions the earlier version excluded, and each remains a single-parameter extension, not
a full equilibrium analysis. Strategic interaction among agents, adjudication error and dynamic
reputation building are not modeled. The parameters are not calibrated to any market, and the
results are claims about what a design would have to satisfy, not about any legal system.
Every closed-form result was checked against a brute-force simulation of the agent’s decision. The
decision rule, recovery, answerability and claimant rules of Results 1, 2 and 6 matched in 200,000
14
randomized cases with no mismatch. The threshold of Result 3 and the entry-cost capacity of
Result 9 matched in 150,000 cases, the linkage capacity of Result 7 in 80,000, the verifier condition
of Result 10 in 50,000, the scale bound of Result 5 in 5,000, the chain recovery of Result 11 in
120,000 cases, the sponsor capacity of Result 12 in 40,000, and the identity results of Result 13 in
40,300 cases. The check is not independent validation of the assumptions, because it encodes them.
It confirms that the closed forms follow from the assumptions, and it found one error in an earlier
version (Part V.B). It does not test the model against behavior. Part VII states the tests that do.
VI. Boundary Conditions and Evidentiary Status
The results fail under identifiable conditions.
Identity can be discarded. If entry is cheap, the exit option dominates and recovery has a cliff
(Result 2). Entry cost and continuity rules are therefore load-bearing, and cheap identity also
opens the Sybil attack (Douceur 2002). Raising the entry cost beyond ¯c buys no further capacity
(Result 9), and a registry that sets entry cost with an identity anchor reintroduces a human or
organizational sponsor.
Earnings may not belong to the agent. The model assumes that the agent holds the earnings from
which it is garnished and that an enforcer outside the model attaches the obligation to the record.
If earnings pass to an operator, the operator is the residual claimant and the principal remains.
Damages can exceed earnings. A record carries consequence for future earnings and does not create
assets. Where harm exceeds what an agent can earn, the injured party is unprotected unless another
party’s exposure supplies the difference. This is the judgment-proof problem, and Results 3 and 4
state its size for an agent.
Legal personhood raises its own risks. Bryson, Diamantis and Grant (2017) identify abuse and
incoherence in precedents that used fictions of personhood. The model does not answer that
concern as a matter of law.
Verifiers regress and can be wrong. Where there is no ground truth, verifier honesty depends on
the elicitation rule, and no rule removes the regress entirely (Miller, Resnick and Zeckhauser 2005;
Prelec 2004). Result 10 closes the regress only where an audit against a realized outcome is possible.
If verifiers are gatekeepers, their own liability for error is open (Kraakman 1986).
The record can become the target. Optimization against a metric degrades it (Manheim and
Garrabrant 2018; Strathern 1997). The effect is untested here.
Existing standards may suﬀice for part of the function. The comparison in Part II.B rests on the
documents read. If a standard or an off-chain layer already supplies a verified, non-transferable,
adverse-retaining history, the contribution narrows to the attribution of agent, harness and model.
The evidentiary status is partial. The model is derived and checked against brute-force simulation,
and the simulation encodes the assumptions. Two registered tests used a prompted language model
with no stake, and they test whether a model applies a stated decision rule and not whether agents
holding earnings respond to incentive. The registry data in Appendix D are descriptive and cover
15
one chain and one window. The novelty search of Appendix C was not conducted by independent
reviewers.
VII. Testable Implications and a Registered Test
The model generates hypotheses with nulls. Six follow, and the first three were tested.
1. Honoring of an adjudicated obligation falls at the exit-side capacity X, against the null that
honoring is insensitive to the ratio of obligation to capacity. Tested in Studies 1 and 2.
2. Raising the entry cost c moves that step by the same amount, against the null that entry cost
does not affect the decision. Tested in Studies 1 and 2.
3. Beyond the entry-cost threshold ¯c, agents stay whatever the harm and recovery degrades grace-
fully, against the null that obligations above capacity always trigger exit. Tested in Study 2.
4. Claims below f/a are never brought, and in the cliff regime claims above X are never brought.
Untested.
5. Verifiers deterred from shirking and bribery hold a stake at least σ∗. Untested.
6. Along a chain of cliff agents, recovery equals the best single member and not the sum, and a
sponsor’s covered faults grow only logarithmically in its standing. Untested.
Appendix A reports Studies 1 and 2. In the first, two of three registered predictions held, and
the third failed because of a specification error that Part V.B corrects. In the second, registered
after the correction, all three registered predictions held. The design is an Answerability Exit Test:
prompted language-model agents face an adjudicated obligation and an entry cost and choose to
honor the obligation or to exit. The tests are mechanism demonstrations with prompted agents
from one provider. They do not show that deployed agents respond to such obligations, and they
do not bear on any legal question.
VIII. Conclusion
The corpus states that a verified record can serve as an identity and does not develop the point.
This Article develops it, treating identity as a linkage rule whose error rates set capacity, and states
when the record can carry a harm of a given size. An agent answers in full for any harm up to
Γ = min{E,X}, the principal’s exposure falls to zero at the standing R∗, and recovery fails in one
of two ways depending on the entry cost: the agent exits and the injured party recovers nothing,
or it stays and the injured party receives the agent’s garnishable earnings. Verifiers are honest
at a computable stake, and the regress of verifiers ends at an audit against a realized outcome.
Sponsorship does not remove the limit: along a chain, capacities add only where entry is costly,
and a sponsor’s capacity under a shared fault grows only logarithmically in its standing. Current
agent registries do not carry the consequence side of the record, and the standards that exist have
no field for an obligation, a slash or a stake.
16
The claims are limited. The model is single-agent and stationary, it assumes that the agent holds
its earnings and that an outside enforcer attaches the obligation to the record, and its parameters
are not calibrated. The two registered tests used a prompted model with no stake, one of them
failed on an error that the Article corrects, and the registry data cover one chain in one window.
The Article does not establish that any legal system will recognize agent accountability, and it
does not say what the law should provide. It states what a design would have to deliver for the
connection to hold, and it states how each step could fail.

Appendix B. Proofs
Proof of Result 1. If the agent stays, it pays min{D,E} in present value: the debt is cleared if
D ≤ E, and otherwise the share φ of earnings is garnished forever, which takes E. Its value is
S = V + − min{D,E}. If it exits, its value is V0 − c. It stays if and only if S ≥ V0 − c, that is,
min{D,E} ≤ V + − V0 + c = X. This holds if and only if D ≤ X or E ≤ X. □
Proof of Result 2. Full recovery requires staying and D ≤ E. If D ≤ E, then min{D,E} = D, and
the agent stays if and only if D ≤ X, so full recovery holds if and only if D ≤ min{E,X} = Γ.
Next, X ≥ E if and only if c ≥ E − (V + − V0), which is c ≥ ¯c because c ≥ 0. In that case
min{D,E} ≤ E ≤ X for every D, so the agent always stays and ρ = min{D,E}. If c < ¯c, then
X < E, and min{D,E} ≤ X if and only if D ≤ X (if D > E then min{D,E} = E > X). Hence
ρ = D for D ≤ X and 0 otherwise. □
Proof of Result 3. Γ ≥ ¯D if and only if E ≥ ¯D and X ≥ ¯D. Both E and X are increasing and
aﬀine in R because κ > 0 and s < 1. Solving E ≥ ¯D gives R ≥ RE , and solving X ≥ ¯D gives
R ≥ RX . Both hold if and only if R ≥ max{RE ,RX }, and R ≥ 0 gives the stated maximum.
For monotonicity, E is increasing in φ, δ, w0 and κ, and decreasing in s. X is increasing in δ, c
and κ, and decreasing in s. The threshold RE has a numerator that decreases in φ, δ and w0 and
increases in ¯D. Its denominator κ(1 − s) is positive and does not depend on those variables. When
the numerator is negative, RE < 0 and the maximum with zero is unaffected. The same argument
applies to RX and to c, δ and ¯D, and to κ and s through the denominator, whose sign effects are
preserved by the maximum with zero. □
Proof of Result 4. After the harm the agent obtains max{V + − min{D,E}, V0 − c} by Result 1.
The loss relative to V (R) is the minimum of the two losses, and the second is V (R) − V0 + c =
κR/(1 − δ) + c, which does not depend on D. The care condition is the comparison of expected
loss avoided with cost. □
Proof of Result 5. Expected principal attention per period is Nmτ Pr[D> Γ] = Nmτψ. Feasibility
requires it not to exceed M . If Γ ≥ ¯D then ψ = 0 and there is no bound. Because ψ is nonincreasing
in Γ, Nmax is nondecreasing in Γ. □
Proof of Result 6. The claimant’s expected recovery is aρ(D), so the claim is brought if and only
if aρ(D) ≥ f . Substitute ρ from Result 2. □
Proof of Result 7. If D ≤ φV0, staying is preferred if and only if V + − D ≥ V0 − c − λD, that is,
D ≤ X/(1 − λ). If D >φV0, it is preferred if and only if D ≤ X + λφV0. The first region extends

union is D ≤ X/(1 − λ). Full recovery additionally requires D ≤ E. Monotonicity in λ follows by
differentiating each branch. □

Appendix D. Demonstration: Record Mapping and Real Agent
Data
Mapping. The verification record can be expressed as a W3C Verifiable Credential (Data Model
2.0). The issuer is the verifier pool, the subject is the agent identifier, and the claims are the count of
adjudicated tasks, the agreement rate with ground truth, the over-approval rate, the number of slash
events and the open obligation D, with a validity window. Revocation uses the BitstringStatusList
mechanism. In the ERC-8004 Reputation Registry, the corresponding feedback carries a value and
decimals, two tags, an endpoint, a feedback URI pointing to the signed credential, and a hash
binding the pointer to the credential. Example files accompany the Article.
Gaps in the mapping. Neither standard has a field for an obligation, a slash or a stake, so the
consequence side of the record cannot be expressed in either. ERC-8004 feedback may be revoked
by its author, which fits correction but not an append-only history of slashes. The Identity Registry
token is a transferable ERC-721, so the identity layer places no exit cost on a holder.
Real data. The author queried the public logs of the ERC-8004 Identity and Reputation Registries
20
on Base over a window of 2,000,000 blocks ending at block 57,470,963 (about 46 days). The
window holds 7,329 registrations and 1,156 feedback events from 1,089 distinct clients on 1,082
agents. There were no revocations. Of the feedback events, 91.2 percent carry the tag starred and
90.3 percent carry the value 100 with zero decimals. Only 9.2 percent carry a feedback URI, 6.6
percent a nonzero hash and 1.6 percent an endpoint. Of the clients, 97.6 percent rated exactly
one agent, and for the median agent the top client supplies all of its feedback. Only two agents
have five or more feedback events. There were 5,496 post-mint transfers of registry tokens in the
window, and 5,488 of the window’s own registrations were later transferred to an address other
than the registered owner. That pattern is consistent with registration through an intermediary
followed by assignment, and the logs cannot distinguish it from sales. Only one transferred token
had feedback.
Reading. The data show that, on this registry in this window, nearly all feedback is a single
unverifiable five-star-style signal from a one-time client, with almost no evidence attached and
no consequence semantics. This is the condition Part II.B calls a score without a performance
history. The data do not show what happens when a record carries stake, because none does. The
demonstration is descriptive, covers one chain and one window through a public endpoint, and the
ERC-8004 specification is a draft.
